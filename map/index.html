Methodology & sources

How this map was built

Where the data comes from

Every number on this map comes directly from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's Safe Drinking Water Information System (SDWIS), the federal database of record for public drinking water compliance. SDWIS is populated by state primacy agencies (MassDEP, CT DPH, RI DOH, VT DEC, ME DWP, NH DES) who receive test results from every regulated water system and forward the enforcement record to EPA.

The exact snapshot used is the EPA ECHO SDWA quarterly download, 2026 Q2 refresh (published July 2026):

  • ECHO SDWA Data Downloads — official EPA distribution page
  • Direct file: SDWA_latest_downloads.zip (457 MB)
  • Tables used: SDWA_PUB_WATER_SYSTEMS, SDWA_VIOLATIONS_ENFORCEMENT, SDWA_GEOGRAPHIC_AREAS, SDWA_REF_CODE_VALUES

Town coordinates come from the U.S. Census Bureau 2022 Gazetteer — both the Places file and the County Subdivisions file (New England "towns" are governed as county subdivisions, not incorporated places, so we join against both).

Measured contamination values

The "measured over a limit" view is not a count of violations — it is the actual sample result the utility reported to EPA, compared to the federal legal limit.

  • Chemical contaminants (arsenic, nitrate, uranium, TTHM, PCE, radium, etc.): from SDWA_VIOLATIONS_ENFORCEMENT. When a health-based violation was filed, the state agency records the exact VIOL_MEASURE reading and the applicable FEDERAL_MCL. Ratio = measured ÷ MCL. Only records with a numeric measure are included — some health violations (surface water treatment failures, revised total coliform) don't report a single value.
  • Lead & copper: from the separate SDWA_LCR_SAMPLES table, which stores 90th-percentile sample results independent of whether a violation was filed. Values in µg/L (ppb) are shown in ppb; the federal action level is 15 ppb for lead and 1.3 mg/L for copper. Exceeding the action level triggers required corrosion-control treatment; it is not by itself a violation but is a strong indicator of pipe or plumbing risk.
A note on the lead action level. The 15 ppb figure used throughout this map is the action level in force for the sample results shown. Under EPA's Lead and Copper Rule Improvements the action level drops to 10 ppb, with a compliance date of 1 November 2027. Systems shown here as below the action level may not be below the one that will apply. 82 towns on this map fall between 10 and 15 ppb — they pass the current level and would not pass the one that replaces it. 40 of those have no violations on file at all.

Dates are monitoring periods, not sample days

This matters for reading any date on this map. The dates attached to these records are the boundaries of the compliance monitoring period a result is filed against — not the day someone actually drew water from a tap.

  • Lead and copper results carry the end of the monitoring period: 90.6% fall on 31 December, and the remainder on 30 June, 30 September or 31 January. The sample was collected at some point inside that window.
  • Measured exceedances carry the start of the period: every one falls on 1 January, 1 April, 1 July or 1 October — quarter boundaries.

So a result labelled "period ending 12/31/2022" was sampled somewhere in the run-up to that date, and the map does not know exactly when. Anywhere a date appears, read it as "the compliance period this result belongs to."

There is no testing calendar here

This map cannot tell you when a town's water was last tested, when it is next due, or what a full round of testing found. It holds violations, plus the single worst lead or copper result and up to four measured exceedances per town. Routine results that came back within their limits are not in the federal violation record at all — they are published by each utility in its annual Consumer Confidence Report, as a PDF, in a layout of its own choosing.

What that means in practice, across the 1,151 towns here:

Towns with any date at all974 of 1,151 (85%). The remaining 177 have no dated record of any kind.
Towns with a lead or copper result735 (64%)
Towns with a measured exceedance79 (7%)
Age of the newest lead resultMedian 3.6 years. 75% are more than 2 years old; 22% more than 5 years.

None of that is a failing of the water systems. Systems in compliance are required to sample lead infrequently — every three years is common, and reduced monitoring can stretch it further — so long gaps are the expected pattern rather than evidence of neglect. It does mean that for most towns on this map, the most recent published number is several years old, and nothing here describes what is coming out of a tap today.

What is excluded

  • Private wells. An estimated 40–50% of New England households drink from private wells that have no federal reporting obligation. Their water quality is not in this map. State well-water programs (e.g. MA Private Well Program, Maine CDC well testing) hold partial data; USGS ambient groundwater surveys hold more.
  • Transient and non-community systems. Campgrounds, rest stops, seasonal restaurants, and other water systems that don't serve the same people year-round are regulated but excluded here because they don't reflect where people live.
  • Bottled water and point-of-use filters. Not in scope.
  • Very recent events. SDWIS is refreshed quarterly and violations are typically reported ~one quarter after they occur, so the freshest events in this snapshot are roughly six months old. Some state agency websites (notably MassDEP and CT DPH) publish more current data.
  • Everything that was never tested. A system reports on the contaminants it is required to monitor. Substances outside that list — for many systems, PFAS among them — do not appear here because nobody measured them, which is not the same as their being absent.

How the compliance index is calculated

Two kinds of violation are counted separately, then combined:

  • Health-based violations — a contaminant sample exceeded the federal Maximum Contaminant Level (MCL) or a treatment technique failed. These are actual exposure events (arsenic, lead, coliform, nitrates, disinfection byproducts, PFAS, etc.). Flagged by SDWIS field IS_HEALTH_BASED_IND = 'Y'.
  • Monitoring & Reporting (M&R) violations — the system did not test on the required schedule or did not report results on time. This is a compliance and transparency failure, not a confirmed exposure. Flagged by VIOLATION_CATEGORY_CODE in ('MR','MON','RPT').

Both describe the compliance record, but health-based violations are weighted more heavily:

raw = (health_violations × 3) + monitoring_violations
intensity = raw / (population_served / 100)
index = min(100, ln(1 + raw) × 15 + ln(1 + intensity) × 10)

The log scale prevents one large utility from dominating and gives smaller towns proportional weight. A town's band is then raised if any measured contamination is severe, so a town with a single 1,820 ppb lead reading is flagged even with no violations on paper:

Repeated violations5+ health-based violations, or index ≥ 60, or any single measurement ≥ 5× the federal MCL or action level
Some violations1+ health violation, or 3+ M&R violations, or index ≥ 30, or a measurement 1.5–5× over a limit
Minor or historicalAny recorded violation below the band above, or lead / a chemical detected but below the action level
No violations recordedNo recorded violations and no measured exceedances in the five-year window
This index describes a compliance record, not a health risk. It measures what a water system reported to its regulator over five years. It is not a measure of how safe anyone's water is, and it says nothing at all about any individual house. A band of "no violations recorded" means a system met its requirements — it does not mean nothing is present in the water, and it does not mean everything was tested for.

What this map cannot tell you

Everything here was measured in a water system, at sample points chosen for regulatory compliance, on particular dates. None of it was measured at any individual tap. Between a treatment plant and a kitchen faucet there is a distribution network, a service line whose material the utility may not know, the building's own plumbing, the fixture itself, and however long the water has been sitting in it. Lead in particular almost never originates at the source — it is picked up on the way. A public record describes the system. Only a sample from a tap describes that tap.

How towns are attributed

SDWIS reports water systems, not towns. To map at the town level, each system is joined to the CITY_SERVED field from the SDWA_GEOGRAPHIC_AREAS table. A water district serving multiple towns is counted for each town it serves — violations and population are attributed to every served town, not divided. This overstates raw totals in overlapping service areas but reflects who is actually drinking the water. Towns are then joined to Census coordinates by name.

Known data quality issues. Some water utilities record cities in the wrong state field (a CT utility labelled "MA"), or use abbreviations ("ST" vs "SAINT", "HBR" vs "HARBOR"). 56 of 1,207 aggregated town rows could not be matched to a Census coordinate and are excluded from the map. These are almost entirely small systems.

Reproducibility

  1. Download the SDWA quarterly ZIP from EPA.
  2. Filter PUB_WATER_SYSTEMS to STATE_CODE in NE, PWS_ACTIVITY_CODE = 'A', PWS_TYPE_CODE = 'CWS'.
  3. Filter VIOLATIONS_ENFORCEMENT to those PWSIDs with NON_COMPL_PER_BEGIN_DATE ≥ 2020-01-01.
  4. Aggregate by CITY_SERVED from GEOGRAPHIC_AREAS. Geocode against Census 2022 Gazetteer cousubs + places.

Every public water system in America is required to report what it finds. That record is free, it is public, and it is written so that you will not read it.

A number that should not exist

Seven thousand three hundred and ninety zeros.

Of the 24,100 lead results filed across New England, 7,390 are recorded as exactly zero. A laboratory does not measure zero. It measures down to a limit and then stops seeing.

A zero on this record means nothing was found above the point where the instrument could still detect it. That is a different statement from none, and a much smaller one. It is also the single most common result in the entire dataset.

This is not misconduct. It is how the reporting works. But it means that a great deal of what looks like reassurance is, more precisely, the absence of a measurement.

What these are, and what they do

Three of them matter more than all the rest.

What follows is what the health agencies say — quoted, attributed, and not stretched a millimetre past what they wrote. Finding one of these in a system's record does not mean it is in your glass. It means the question is open.

Lead

No safe level in children

Lead is almost never present in water at its source. It is collected on the journey — from the service line beneath the street, from solder in older joints, from the brass of the tap itself. Which makes it a property of your building, not of your town.

It is the reason the federal action level falls from 15 parts per billion to 10 in November 2027, and the reason 564 New England systems currently sitting between those two numbers are about to find themselves on the wrong side of a line that did not move under them so much as arrive.

In children, lead exposure is associated with damage to the developing brain and nervous system, and with slowed growth and development. No safe blood lead level has been identified.

U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Childhood Lead Poisoning Prevention. U.S. EPA, Lead and Copper Rule Improvements.

Nitrate

Acute · infants under six months

Nitrate is the one contaminant here with a fast danger rather than a slow one, and it is specific to the smallest person in the house.

The sentence opposite is not our characterisation. It is the wording a water utility is legally required to print when it exceeds the limit. If you prepare formula with tap water, this is the number to know.

Infants below the age of six months who drink water containing nitrate in excess of the maximum contaminant level could become seriously ill and, if untreated, may die.

U.S. EPA, National Primary Drinking Water Regulations — mandatory health effects language for nitrate.

PFAS

Persistent · half of systems

PFAS do not meaningfully break down — not in soil, not in water, not in a body. They accumulate. In 2024 the EPA set the first national limits for several of them at four parts per trillion, a threshold so low that the standard laboratory method cannot reliably see beneath it.

Two caveats, and they cut in opposite directions. Ninety-seven per cent of individual results found nothing at all. And the limits themselves are moving: in May 2026 the EPA proposed rescinding several of the 2024 standards and extending the deadline for the remainder to 2031. What is legal is in flux. What is present is not.

Half of the New England systems in the national monitoring programme — 243 of 485 — reported at least one detection.

U.S. EPA, PFAS National Primary Drinking Water Regulation (2024) and proposed rules of 20 May 2026. EPA UCMR 5 occurrence data, February 2026 release.

Why children are not small adults

The same water, concentrated into the smallest person in the house.

A bottle-fed infant drinks a great deal of water relative to their size — far more, pound for pound, than anyone else at the table. Formula is made with it. It is very nearly all they consume.

Their bodies are also still being built. That is why health agencies treat an identical concentration differently depending on who is drinking it, and why the guidance on lead is written specifically about children rather than about people.

What we are not saying

We are not telling you your child has been harmed. Nothing on this page can tell you that, and anyone who claims it from a public record is guessing.

What we are saying is narrower, and we think harder to put down: the people most affected by whatever is in your water are the least able to tell you that anything is wrong — and in most New England towns, no one has measured it in three and a half years.

That is not an accusation. Systems in compliance are required to sample infrequently, and good performance earns longer gaps. The record is behaving exactly as designed. The question is simply what has happened since.

The part nobody mentions

None of it was measured at your tap.

Every figure on this page was measured in a water system — at sampling points chosen for regulatory compliance, on particular dates. Between the treatment plant and your kitchen there are four further stages, and no one samples any of them.

This is not a gap anyone is concealing. It is simply how drinking water is regulated: systems are monitored at the level of the system. A public record describes the system. Only a sample from your tap describes your tap.

Who we are.

Mothers of Water started with parents going looking for their own town's water record, finding it close to unreadable, and deciding to read all of it anyway. Every figure on this site is public. Every source is named below. You do not have to take our word for any of it, and we would rather you checked.

We also arrange water testing, and we earn money when a household we have tested decides to buy treatment from a provider we introduce. That is what pays for this, and it is why the map is free. We would rather tell you now than have you find out later.

What this cannot tell you

It cannot tell you what is in the water at your address. It cannot tell you that anyone has been harmed. It cannot tell you that a system with no violations is safe — only that nothing was filed.

Roughly one household in five in New England draws from a private well. No reporting requirement exists for those, so no record of any kind exists either.
